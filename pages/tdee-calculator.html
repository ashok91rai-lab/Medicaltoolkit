What is TDEE and Why Is It the Most Important Calorie Number?

Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) is the total number of calories your body burns each day, accounting for everything from breathing (your basal metabolic rate) to exercise, digestion (the thermic effect of food), and non-exercise activity thermogenesis (NEAT — fidgeting, walking, standing). TDEE is the single most important calorie number for body composition goals: eating at TDEE maintains weight, eating below it loses fat, eating above it gains muscle (and fat). Unlike BMR, TDEE gives you a real-world calorie target that reflects your lifestyle. This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor BMR equation — validated as the most accurate for diverse populations — multiplied by an activity factor derived from your weekly exercise habits.

How to Use the TDEE Calculator

  • Step 1 — Enter your age, sex, height, and weight: These are used to calculate your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation.
  • Step 2 — Select your activity level: Choose the option that most accurately reflects your typical weekly activity. Most people underestimate their sedentary time — be honest for an accurate result.
  • Step 3 — Click Calculate to see your BMR, TDEE, and calorie recommendations for weight loss, maintenance, and weight gain.

Use your TDEE as your calorie baseline. Eat at TDEE to maintain weight. Create a 300–500 calorie deficit to lose weight at 0.5–1 lb per week. Add 250–500 calories to gain weight (primarily muscle with proper training).

How TDEE Is Calculated — The Mifflin-St Jeor Equation

This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation (1990), which is considered the most accurate BMR formula for most adults according to the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics:

BMR (Men): (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5

BMR (Women): (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161

TDEE = BMR × Activity Multiplier:
Sedentary (desk job, little exercise): × 1.2
Lightly Active (light exercise 1–3 days/week): × 1.375
Moderately Active (moderate exercise 3–5 days/week): × 1.55
Very Active (hard exercise 6–7 days/week): × 1.725
Extremely Active (physical job + intense exercise daily): × 1.9

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation typically estimates BMR within 10% of measured values for most individuals. People with significantly above or below average muscle mass (competitive athletes, elderly individuals with sarcopenia) may find their actual needs differ from the estimate.

Using Your TDEE for Weight Management

Your TDEE is the single most important number for achieving any body composition goal:

  • Weight Loss — Eat 300–500 calories below TDEE: This creates a deficit of 0.5–1 lb of fat loss per week without excessive muscle loss. Aggressive deficits above 750 calories/day significantly increase muscle catabolism, slow metabolism, and are difficult to sustain.
  • Weight Maintenance — Eat at TDEE: Consuming calories equal to your TDEE stabilizes weight. Adjust TDEE periodically as your weight changes (because a lighter body burns fewer calories at rest).
  • Lean Muscle Gain ("Lean Bulk") — Eat 200–300 calories above TDEE: A modest surplus combined with progressive resistance training maximizes muscle growth while minimizing fat gain. Larger surpluses result in proportionally more fat gain.
  • Body Recomposition — Eat at or slightly below TDEE: With high protein intake (0.8–1g/lb bodyweight) and consistent resistance training, it is possible to simultaneously lose fat and gain muscle, especially in beginners and those returning from a training break.

Maximizing Metabolic Rate — Evidence-Based Strategies

  • Build Muscle: Each pound of muscle burns approximately 6–7 calories/day at rest vs. 2 calories/day for fat. Over years of training, substantial metabolic increases are possible.
  • Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis (NEAT): Casual movement throughout the day (fidgeting, standing, walking, taking stairs) can burn 200–1,000 extra calories/day. Deliberately increasing daily steps from 5,000 to 10,000 creates a meaningful caloric difference.
  • Avoid Severe Calorie Restriction: Eating below 1,200 calories/day (women) or 1,500 calories/day (men) triggers adaptive thermogenesis — your body reduces TDEE by reducing non-essential metabolic activity, making further weight loss progressively harder.
  • Diet Breaks: If dieting for 8–12+ weeks, incorporating a 1–2 week "maintenance break" has been shown to reduce adaptive thermogenesis and improve long-term weight loss outcomes.
  • High-Protein Diet: Protein has a thermic effect of 20–30% (meaning you burn 20–30% of protein calories just digesting it), compared to 5–10% for carbohydrates and 0–3% for fat. High protein diets modestly increase TDEE.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between BMR and TDEE?

BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate) is the calories burned at complete rest — just to keep you alive. TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) is BMR multiplied by your activity level and adds calories burned through all daily movement, exercise, and digestion. TDEE is always higher than BMR.

How do I choose the right activity level?

Be honest and realistic. "Sedentary" means a desk job with little walking. "Light" means 1–3 gym sessions per week. "Moderate" means 4–5 sessions or an active job. "Very active" is daily intense training. "Extra active" is professional athletes or those with very physically demanding jobs plus training.

Why am I not losing weight at a 500-calorie deficit?

Possible reasons: underestimated calorie intake (food labels can be inaccurate by up to 20%), overestimated activity level, metabolic adaptation (body adjusts after sustained deficits), insufficient protein causing muscle loss, poor sleep affecting hormones, or water retention masking fat loss. Recalculate after 4–6 weeks and adjust based on actual results.

🔥 Metabolism Tips

Recalculate after weight change For every 10 lbs you lose, your TDEE drops by approximately 50–70 calories. Recalculate monthly to keep your targets accurate.
Weekend eating matters Many people undereat on weekdays and overeat on weekends, canceling their weekly deficit. Consistent daily intake outperforms extreme variation.
Cardio vs. weights for fat loss Cardio burns more calories during the session; weight training burns more calories after (excess post-exercise oxygen consumption). Both are valuable — combine them.
