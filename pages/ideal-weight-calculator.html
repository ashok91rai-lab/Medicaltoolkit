What is Ideal Body Weight and How Is It Calculated?

Ideal Body Weight (IBW) is a clinical estimate of a person's optimal weight based on height and sex, originally developed for drug dosing purposes rather than aesthetic goals. The three most widely used formulas are the Devine formula (1974), the Robinson formula (1983), and the Miller formula (1983). Each uses height in inches above 5 feet as the base variable. These formulas provide useful clinical benchmarks but have limitations: they don't account for muscle mass, age, ethnicity, or individual body composition. They work best as one data point alongside BMI, body fat percentage, and your doctor's assessment.

How to Use the Ideal Weight Calculator

The Four Ideal Weight Formulas

This calculator computes ideal body weight using four clinically established formulas:

Devine Formula (1974):
Men: IBW = 50 kg + 2.3 kg × (height in inches − 60)
Women: IBW = 45.5 kg + 2.3 kg × (height in inches − 60)

Robinson Formula (1983):
Men: IBW = 52 kg + 1.9 kg × (height in inches − 60)
Women: IBW = 49 kg + 1.7 kg × (height in inches − 60)

Miller Formula (1983):
Men: IBW = 56.2 kg + 1.41 kg × (height in inches − 60)
Women: IBW = 53.1 kg + 1.36 kg × (height in inches − 60)

Hamwi Formula (1964):
Men: IBW = 48 kg + 2.7 kg × (height in inches − 60)
Women: IBW = 45.4 kg + 2.27 kg × (height in inches − 60)

The Devine formula is most widely used in clinical settings, particularly for calculating medication dosages based on ideal body weight. The healthy BMI range (18.5–24.9) provides an alternative view of healthy weight relative to height.

Understanding Ideal Weight vs. Healthy Weight

Ideal weight formulas provide a mathematical estimate based on height and sex alone. They do not account for age, muscle mass, bone density, ethnicity, or fitness level. Here is how to interpret the results practically:

  • If your weight is within any of the formula ranges: You are likely in a healthy weight zone. Focus on body composition (muscle-to-fat ratio) and fitness rather than the scale number.
  • If your weight is above all formula ranges: Consider consulting a registered dietitian or physician to discuss a healthy, sustainable approach to weight management. Gradual loss of 0.5–1 kg/week is the evidence-based target.
  • If your weight is below all formula ranges: Underweight status can indicate nutritional deficiencies or health conditions. Gaining weight through nutrient-dense foods and resistance training (to build lean mass) is recommended under medical guidance.
  • Muscular individuals: Athletes and those with significant muscle mass may exceed ideal weight ranges while being perfectly healthy. In these cases, body fat percentage is a far more meaningful metric.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which ideal weight formula is most accurate?

No single formula is universally most accurate, as each was developed in different clinical contexts. The Devine formula is most commonly used in clinical drug dosing. Using the average of all three gives a more balanced estimate. BMI and body fat percentage provide additional context.

Do these formulas apply to everyone?

These formulas are best suited to adults of average build. They may underestimate ideal weight for athletes with high muscle mass, and do not account for age (muscle mass typically decreases with age). They are reference tools, not diagnostic criteria.

Should I aim to reach my ideal weight?

Not necessarily. These are statistical averages, not personalised targets. A more meaningful goal is achieving a healthy body fat percentage and BMI. Small amounts of weight loss (5–10%) in overweight individuals produce significant health benefits regardless of reaching an "ideal" weight.
