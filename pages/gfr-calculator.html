What is eGFR and Why Does It Matter?

The estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR) is the best overall index of kidney function and is used to diagnose, stage, and monitor chronic kidney disease (CKD). GFR measures how much blood the kidneys filter per minute per 1.73 m² of body surface area. The CKD-EPI 2021 equation uses serum creatinine, age, and sex to estimate GFR without a race variable, making it the current standard recommended by the American Society of Nephrology and NKF. A declining eGFR over time is a key indicator of kidney disease progression and guides treatment decisions from lifestyle modification to dialysis planning.

How to Use the GFR Calculator

This calculator uses the CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) 2021 equation — the most current and accurate formula recommended by the National Kidney Foundation (NKF) and the American Society of Nephrology (ASN).

  • Step 1 — Enter your serum creatinine level: This is found on your blood test report. Enter the value and select your unit (mg/dL is standard in the USA; μmol/L is used in Canada, UK, and Australia).
  • Step 2 — Enter your age in years. GFR naturally declines with age — the formula accounts for this.
  • Step 3 — Select your sex: Muscle mass differences between sexes affect creatinine production, which affects the formula result.
  • Step 4 — Click "Calculate eGFR" to instantly see your estimated GFR value and your CKD stage if applicable.

Always have your results interpreted by a healthcare professional alongside other kidney function tests such as urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and a comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP).

The CKD-EPI Formula — How eGFR is Calculated

The CKD-EPI 2021 equation estimates kidney filtration capacity using serum creatinine, age, and sex. The 2021 revision removed the race variable (previously used in older equations) after research showed it was not biologically justified and was contributing to healthcare inequity.

CKD-EPI 2021 (Race-Free) Formula:

eGFR = 142 × min(Scr/κ, 1)^α × max(Scr/κ, 1)^(−1.200) × 0.9938^Age

× 1.012 [if female]

Where: κ = 0.7 (female) or 0.9 (male)
α = −0.241 (female) or −0.302 (male)
Scr = serum creatinine in mg/dL

The result is expressed in mL/min/1.73m², representing the volume of blood filtered by the kidneys per minute, normalized to standard body surface area.

GFR Results and CKD Staging

The National Kidney Foundation defines five stages of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) based on eGFR:

  • Stage G1 — eGFR ≥90: Normal or high kidney function. If eGFR is ≥90 with markers of kidney damage (protein in urine), this is Stage G1 CKD.
  • Stage G2 — eGFR 60–89: Mildly reduced function. Often no symptoms. Monitor annually with urine tests.
  • Stage G3a — eGFR 45–59: Mildly to moderately reduced. Begin dietary adjustments (limit phosphorus, potassium if elevated). See a nephrologist.
  • Stage G3b — eGFR 30–44: Moderately to severely reduced. Medication management of complications (anemia, bone disease) begins.
  • Stage G4 — eGFR 15–29: Severely reduced. Begin planning for kidney replacement therapy (dialysis or transplant).
  • Stage G5 — eGFR <15: Kidney failure. Dialysis or kidney transplant is required to sustain life.

A single eGFR value is not sufficient to diagnose CKD. Diagnosis requires two abnormal readings at least 3 months apart, as eGFR can fluctuate based on hydration, diet, medications, and acute illness.

How to Protect Your Kidney Function

  • Control Blood Pressure: Target below 130/80 mmHg. Hypertension is the second leading cause of kidney failure after diabetes. ACE inhibitors or ARBs are often prescribed to protect kidneys specifically.
  • Manage Blood Sugar: Diabetic nephropathy accounts for 44% of new kidney failure cases in the USA. Keeping HbA1c below 7% dramatically slows kidney disease progression.
  • Stay Hydrated: Drink 6–8 glasses of water daily (unless fluid-restricted by your doctor). Chronic mild dehydration concentrates toxins in the kidneys.
  • Avoid NSAIDs: Ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin) and naproxen (Aleve) reduce blood flow to the kidneys. Regular use accelerates kidney decline in those with CKD.
  • Limit Sodium & Protein: High sodium raises BP and stresses kidneys. High-protein diets increase metabolic waste (urea) that kidneys must filter. Consult a renal dietitian for CKD-specific diet guidance.
  • Don't Smoke: Smoking accelerates kidney disease progression and is an independent risk factor for developing CKD.
  • Get Annual Kidney Tests: If you have diabetes, hypertension, a family history of kidney disease, or are over 60, ask your doctor for annual eGFR and urine albumin testing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a normal eGFR?

An eGFR of 60 or higher is generally considered normal. eGFR above 90 is Stage G1 (Normal or High). Between 60–89 is G2 (Mildly Decreased). Below 60 for 3 months or more indicates chronic kidney disease.

Can eGFR be improved?

Yes, especially in early stages. Controlling blood pressure (target below 130/80), managing blood sugar in diabetics, reducing protein intake if advised, stopping NSAIDs, avoiding nephrotoxic medications, and quitting smoking can slow or sometimes partially reverse CKD progression.

Why does the CKD-EPI 2021 equation not include race?

The 2021 update removed the race coefficient from the original 2009 equation after research showed it led to underestimation of CKD in Black patients and could delay diagnosis and treatment. The race-free version performs comparably across diverse populations.
