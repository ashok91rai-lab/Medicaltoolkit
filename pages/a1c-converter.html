Convert A1C to Average Glucose

The A1C test, also known as glycated hemoglobin or HbA1c, measures average blood sugar levels over 2-3 months. This A1C converter allows you to easily convert between A1C percentage and estimated average glucose (eAG). Understanding this relationship helps people with diabetes or prediabetes monitor their long-term blood sugar control. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) provides specific targets for A1C levels based on individual health conditions. This converter uses the standard conversion formula to calculate eAG from A1C and vice versa, supporting both mg/dL and mmol/L units for international use.

How It Works — The Formula

A1C to eAG (mg/dL): eAG = (28.7 × A1C) - 46.7

eAG to A1C: A1C = (eAG + 46.7) / 28.7

Convert mg/dL to mmol/L: mmol/L = mg/dL ÷ 18.01

The conversion formula between A1C and eAG was developed based on research published in Diabetes Care. The relationship is linear, making it easy to convert between the two measurements. A1C reflects average blood glucose over 2-3 months, while eAG (estimated average glucose) expresses this as a daily average glucose value.

Understanding Your Results

A1C Levels — What Do They Mean?

HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) reflects the average blood glucose level over the past 2–3 months. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) defines the following clinical thresholds:

Conversion Formula (ADAG Study, 2008):
eAG (mg/dL) = (28.7 × A1C%) − 46.7
eAG (mmol/L) = (1.594 × A1C%) − 2.594

Example: A1C of 7.0% → eAG = (28.7 × 7.0) − 46.7 = 154 mg/dL (8.6 mmol/L)
  • Below 5.7% — Normal: No diabetes or prediabetes. Annual monitoring is still recommended for people with risk factors (obesity, family history, gestational diabetes history).
  • 5.7–6.4% — Prediabetes: Blood sugar is higher than normal but not yet diabetic. Lifestyle intervention (weight loss, diet, exercise) can prevent or significantly delay progression to type 2 diabetes. The CDC's National Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) is highly effective at this stage.
  • 6.5% and above — Diabetes: Diagnosis requires two abnormal A1C tests or one A1C plus one other diagnostic test (fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL or 2-hour glucose ≥200 mg/dL during an oral glucose tolerance test).
  • Target for people with diabetes — Below 7%: The ADA general target for most non-pregnant adults with diabetes is HbA1c below 7.0%. More stringent targets (below 6.5%) may be appropriate for some younger patients; less stringent targets (below 8%) for older adults or those at risk of hypoglycemia.

How to Lower Your A1C — Evidence-Based Strategies

  • Dietary changes: The Mediterranean diet, low-glycemic index diet, and very low-carbohydrate diet have all demonstrated significant A1C reductions (0.5–1.5%) in clinical trials. Reduce refined carbohydrates, added sugars, and sweetened beverages. Prioritize non-starchy vegetables, legumes, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats.
  • Physical Activity: Both aerobic exercise (walking, cycling, swimming) and resistance training reduce insulin resistance and lower blood glucose. The ADA recommends 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity plus 2–3 resistance training sessions per week. Even a 10-minute walk after meals significantly blunts post-meal glucose spikes.
  • Weight Loss: A 5–10% reduction in body weight produces meaningful A1C reductions (0.5–1.0%) and can sometimes achieve diabetes remission in type 2 diabetes, especially when the condition is recently diagnosed.
  • Medication Adherence: For those prescribed diabetes medications (metformin, GLP-1 agonists, SGLT-2 inhibitors, insulin), consistent adherence dramatically improves A1C control. Never adjust or discontinue medications without consulting your endocrinologist.
  • Sleep and Stress Management: Poor sleep (below 7 hours) and chronic stress elevate cortisol, which raises blood glucose and insulin resistance. Addressing sleep disorders (especially sleep apnea, which is highly prevalent in type 2 diabetes) can improve A1C by 0.5–1.0%.
  • Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM): Using a CGM device provides real-time glucose data that helps identify which foods, activities, and behaviors most impact your glucose control, enabling far more precise diabetes self-management than occasional finger-stick testing alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

A1C measures the percentage of hemoglobin (a protein in red blood cells) that has glucose attached to it. This percentage reflects your average blood glucose levels over the previous 2-3 months. It's a key indicator of long-term blood sugar control and is used to diagnose diabetes and monitor how well diabetes is being managed.

The conversion formula is quite accurate for most people, with an average error of about ±5-10 mg/dL. However, accuracy can vary based on individual factors including age, ethnicity, hemoglobin variants, and kidney function. The eAG is an estimate based on population studies, so your actual average glucose may differ slightly from the calculated eAG.

Fasting blood glucose measures your blood sugar at one specific moment (usually in the morning), while A1C reflects your average glucose over 2-3 months. Both are useful: fasting glucose shows how your body manages glucose immediately, while A1C provides a longer-term picture of overall diabetes control. They measure different things and serve different diagnostic purposes.

The American Diabetes Association recommends A1C testing at least twice yearly for people meeting treatment goals, and quarterly testing for those whose therapy has changed or who aren't meeting their targets. Some healthcare providers may recommend more frequent testing during diabetes diagnosis or medication adjustments to ensure optimal management.
