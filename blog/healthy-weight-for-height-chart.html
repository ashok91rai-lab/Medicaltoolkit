How the Healthy Weight Chart Works

This height-weight chart is based on a Body Mass Index (BMI) range of 18.5–24.9, which the World Health Organization (WHO) and the CDC define as "normal" or "healthy" weight for adults. The ranges provided represent the minimum and maximum weight associated with a healthy BMI for each height. These are general guidelines — individual health depends on many factors beyond weight alone, including body composition, fitness level, and metabolic health markers.

How to use this chart: Find your height in the left column. The weight range listed shows the minimum (BMI 18.5) and maximum (BMI 24.9) for a healthy weight at that height. These ranges apply to both men and women, though individual ideal weights differ slightly due to differences in muscle mass and bone density.

How BMI Is Calculated

Metric: BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height² (m²) Imperial: BMI = [weight (lbs) ÷ height² (inches²)] × 703 Example: 5'6" (168cm), 140 lbs (63.5 kg) BMI = 63.5 ÷ (1.68 × 1.68) = 63.5 ÷ 2.82 = 22.5 ✅ Normal

BMI is a population-level screening tool, not a definitive health measure. It does not distinguish muscle from fat, does not account for fat distribution, and can misclassify athletic or muscular individuals. Use it as one data point alongside waist circumference, body fat percentage, and metabolic lab values.

Beyond the Scale: What Else Matters

Two people can weigh exactly the same and have dramatically different health profiles. The following additional metrics are often more meaningful than weight alone:

  • Waist Circumference: Measures visceral fat (abdominal organ fat), the most metabolically dangerous type. Elevated risk: above 35 inches (89 cm) for women, above 40 inches (102 cm) for men. This measurement is strongly associated with type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic syndrome regardless of BMI.
  • Body Fat Percentage: Healthy range is 21–33% for women and 8–19% for men (ACE standards). Two people at the same BMI can differ by 15+ percentage points in body fat — a crucial difference in health risk.
  • Waist-to-Height Ratio (WHtR): Divide your waist circumference by your height. A WHtR above 0.5 indicates excess central adiposity — considered a better cardiovascular risk predictor than BMI alone.
  • Metabolic Health Markers: Fasting blood sugar, blood pressure, triglycerides, and HDL cholesterol give the most complete picture of metabolic health, independent of weight.

Evidence-Based Tips to Achieve a Healthy Weight

  • Sustainable deficit: A 300–500 calorie daily deficit leads to 0.5–1 lb loss per week without triggering adaptive metabolic slowdown. This is the evidence-based "safe" rate.
  • High protein intake: 0.7–1.0g of protein per pound of body weight preserves muscle mass during weight loss, increases satiety, and has the highest thermic effect (burns 20–30% of calories consumed).
  • Resistance training: Building muscle increases resting metabolic rate. A pound of muscle burns approximately 3x more calories at rest than a pound of fat.
  • Prioritize sleep: Sleeping less than 7 hours increases hunger hormones (ghrelin +15%), decreases satiety hormones (leptin -15%), and significantly increases cravings for high-calorie foods.
  • Fiber-rich diet: 25–38g of fiber daily increases fullness, slows digestion, and reduces overall caloric intake without hunger. Focus on vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and fruits.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I weigh for my height?
Your healthy weight range depends on your height and is based on a BMI of 18.5–24.9. For example, a person who is 5'6" (168 cm) has a healthy weight range of approximately 115–154 lbs (52–70 kg). Use our Ideal Weight Calculator for a precise calculation based on your exact measurements, or consult your doctor for a personalized target.
Is BMI the best way to determine healthy weight?
BMI is a useful screening tool but has significant limitations — it does not account for muscle mass, bone density, fat distribution, age, or sex differences. Athletes may have a high BMI despite being lean. For a more complete assessment, use BMI alongside waist circumference (below 35 in women / 40 in men), body fat percentage, and metabolic health markers.
What is overweight for a 5'4" woman?
For a woman who is 5'4" (163 cm), a healthy weight range is approximately 108–145 lbs (49–66 kg). Overweight (BMI 25–29.9) starts at approximately 145 lbs (66 kg), and obesity (BMI 30+) begins at approximately 175 lbs (79 kg). These are guidelines based on BMI — individual health assessment with a doctor provides a more personalized picture.
How can I lose weight to reach my healthy weight range?
The most evidence-based approach is creating a modest calorie deficit of 300–500 calories below your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE), combined with high protein intake to preserve muscle, resistance training 2–3 times per week, and adequate sleep (7–9 hours). Aim for 0.5–1 lb of loss per week for sustainable results. Avoid very low calorie diets below 1,200 cal/day (women) or 1,500 cal/day (men) without medical supervision.
