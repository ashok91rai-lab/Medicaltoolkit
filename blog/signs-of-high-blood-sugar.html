14 Signs of High Blood Sugar (Hyperglycemia) You Shouldn't Ignore

Learn the 14 warning signs of high blood sugar (hyperglycemia) including excessive thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, and blurred vision. Know when to seek medical care.

What Is Hyperglycemia (High Blood Sugar)?

Hyperglycemia — literally "high blood sugar" — occurs when blood glucose levels rise above the normal range. For people without diabetes, the body automatically regulates blood sugar through insulin. When this regulation fails (due to insufficient insulin, insulin resistance, or both), glucose accumulates in the bloodstream rather than entering cells for energy. A fasting blood sugar above 125 mg/dL (6.9 mmol/L) or a post-meal level above 180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L) is considered hyperglycemia in the context of diabetes management. Even people without diagnosed diabetes can experience temporary hyperglycemia due to illness, stress, medication, or poor diet. Recognizing the signs early is critical — chronic elevated blood sugar damages virtually every system in the body.

Emergency signs (call 911): Fruity/acetone breath, extreme confusion, rapid deep breathing, vomiting, severe abdominal pain, or loss of consciousness — these may indicate Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) or Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS), both life-threatening emergencies.

14 Warning Signs of High Blood Sugar

1. Excessive Thirst (Polydipsia)

One of the most classic symptoms. When blood sugar rises, kidneys work overtime to filter excess glucose — pulling water from tissues in the process. This causes dehydration, triggering intense, unrelenting thirst that isn't satisfied by normal fluid intake. This thirst is qualitatively different from ordinary thirst — it persists despite drinking.

2. Frequent Urination (Polyuria)

Directly linked to polydipsia. Kidneys excrete excess glucose in urine, pulling large amounts of water with it through osmotic diuresis. People may urinate every 1–2 hours, including multiple times at night (nocturia). A single voiding may be unusually large in volume.

3. Extreme Fatigue

Despite abundant glucose in the blood, cells cannot absorb it without insulin. Cells are effectively "starving" even as blood sugar is elevated. This cellular energy deficit — combined with dehydration — causes profound, sometimes debilitating fatigue that sleep doesn't relieve.

4. Blurred Vision

High blood sugar draws fluid from the eye lens through osmosis, changing its shape and affecting its ability to focus. Vision becomes blurry, hazy, or fluctuates throughout the day. This is distinct from diabetic retinopathy (which develops over years) — acute blurred vision from hyperglycemia can resolve when blood sugar normalizes.

5. Headaches

Dehydration and the metabolic effects of elevated blood sugar commonly cause headaches. They tend to be dull, persistent, and worse in the morning. High blood sugar headaches often don't respond well to over-the-counter pain relievers.

6. Slow-Healing Wounds and Infections

Elevated glucose impairs white blood cell function, reduces blood flow to small vessels, and damages nerves — creating ideal conditions for infections to develop and wounds to heal poorly. Cuts, scratches, or sores that take weeks to heal (especially on feet and legs) are a significant warning sign. Frequent skin, urinary tract, or vaginal infections also indicate chronically elevated blood sugar.

7. Increased Hunger (Polyphagia)

The triad of polydipsia, polyuria, and polyphagia ("three polys") are classic diabetes warning signs. Despite eating, cells aren't receiving glucose (due to insulin problems), so hunger signals remain active. People may eat large amounts without feeling satisfied.

8. Unexplained Weight Loss

When cells can't use glucose for energy, the body breaks down fat and muscle for fuel — causing weight loss despite eating normally or more than usual. This is particularly pronounced in type 1 diabetes but can occur in undiagnosed type 2 as well.

9. Tingling or Numbness (Neuropathy)

Chronically elevated blood sugar damages peripheral nerves, causing tingling, numbness, burning, or pain — typically starting in the feet and hands. Even short periods of very high blood sugar can cause transient nerve symptoms. Diabetic neuropathy affects approximately 50% of people with long-standing diabetes.

10. Fruity-Smelling Breath

When cells break down fat for energy (due to lack of insulin), they produce ketones as a byproduct. Ketones have a distinctive fruity or acetone-like smell on the breath. This is a serious sign — particularly in people with type 1 diabetes — that may indicate DKA (Diabetic Ketoacidosis), a life-threatening emergency.

11. Nausea and Vomiting

High blood sugar slows gastric emptying (gastroparesis) and — if ketones are building up — directly causes nausea and vomiting. Vomiting makes blood sugar management much more difficult and can rapidly lead to dehydration and worsening hyperglycemia.

12. Difficulty Concentrating ("Brain Fog")

The brain requires a steady supply of glucose. Paradoxically, in hyperglycemia, brain cells may be glucose-deprived if insulin is absent. High blood sugar also causes inflammation and oxidative stress in brain tissue. The result: difficulty concentrating, memory problems, mental sluggishness, and brain fog.

13. Dry Mouth and Skin

Dehydration caused by osmotic diuresis affects all mucous membranes and skin. Dry, cracked lips, dry mouth, and dry or itchy skin — particularly on the lower legs — are common findings in hyperglycemia and uncontrolled diabetes.

14. Recurrent Yeast Infections

Candida yeast thrives in glucose-rich environments. Both men and women with elevated blood sugar frequently experience genital yeast infections, oral thrush, or skin yeast infections in skin folds. Recurrent yeast infections in a non-diabetic person should prompt blood sugar screening.

What Causes High Blood Sugar?

In people with diabetes, common triggers of hyperglycemia include: eating more carbohydrates than planned, skipping or reducing insulin/medication doses, illness or infection (which raises cortisol and stress hormones), physical or emotional stress, less physical activity than usual, and certain medications (corticosteroids, some antipsychotics, diuretics). In people without diagnosed diabetes, hyperglycemia can be triggered by severe illness, surgery, certain medications, hormonal disorders, and pancreatic disease.

Serious Complications of Prolonged Hyperglycemia

Long-term: Heart disease (2–4× higher risk), stroke, kidney failure (diabetic nephropathy), blindness (diabetic retinopathy), nerve damage (neuropathy), and lower limb amputations. These complications develop silently over years of poorly controlled blood sugar.

Short-term emergencies: Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA — primarily type 1 diabetes) and Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS — primarily type 2 diabetes) are acute, potentially fatal complications of very high blood sugar. Both require immediate emergency medical treatment.

How to Lower High Blood Sugar

  • For diagnosed diabetics: Follow your prescribed medication or insulin protocol. Never adjust insulin doses without guidance from your diabetes care team.
  • Hydration: Drink water to help kidneys flush excess glucose. Avoid sugary drinks.
  • Light exercise: A 15–20 minute walk can significantly lower post-meal glucose in people without DKA. Do not exercise if blood sugar is above 240 mg/dL and ketones are present — exercise raises ketone levels in this situation.
  • Identify the trigger: Did you eat more carbs than usual? Skip medication? Are you getting sick? Identifying and addressing the cause prevents recurrence.

Prevention Strategies

  • Follow a low-glycemic, high-fiber diet — emphasizing vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and lean protein
  • Exercise regularly — both cardio and resistance training improve insulin sensitivity
  • Maintain a healthy weight — even 5–10% weight loss significantly improves insulin resistance
  • Monitor blood sugar regularly if you have risk factors
  • Take prescribed medications consistently
  • Manage stress through sleep, mindfulness, and social support

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the first signs of high blood sugar?
The earliest and most common signs of high blood sugar (hyperglycemia) are the classic 'three polys': polydipsia (excessive thirst that isn't relieved by drinking), polyuria (frequent urination, often in large volumes), and polyphagia (increased hunger despite eating). Fatigue, blurred vision, and headaches also appear early. These symptoms can develop gradually over days or weeks.
What blood sugar level is dangerous?
For people with diabetes, blood sugar above 240 mg/dL (13.3 mmol/L) warrants checking for ketones. Above 300 mg/dL (16.7 mmol/L) is a medical emergency requiring immediate care. Even for people without diabetes, blood sugar consistently above 126 mg/dL fasting or 200 mg/dL after meals indicates diabetes and requires prompt medical evaluation.
Can high blood sugar cause headaches?
Yes. Headaches are a common symptom of hyperglycemia, caused primarily by dehydration from osmotic diuresis and the metabolic effects of elevated blood glucose. They tend to be dull and persistent. Headaches from high blood sugar often improve when blood glucose is corrected and hydration is restored.
How quickly can high blood sugar be lowered?
Mild to moderate hyperglycemia in people with type 2 diabetes can be lowered within 1–2 hours through a combination of insulin or medication (for those on treatment), light physical activity (a 15–20 minute walk), and water intake. Severely elevated blood sugar (above 300 mg/dL) requires medical attention. Do not exercise if DKA is suspected.
