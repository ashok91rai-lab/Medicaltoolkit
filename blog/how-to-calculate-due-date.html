How to Calculate Your Due Date: 4 Methods Explained

Learn how to calculate your pregnancy due date using last menstrual period, conception date, IVF transfer date, and ultrasound. Step-by-step guide with examples.

Method 1: Last Menstrual Period (LMP) — Most Common

The most widely used method for calculating a due date is based on the first day of your Last Menstrual Period (LMP). This method is used by virtually all healthcare providers as the starting point for pregnancy dating. It assumes a standard 28-day menstrual cycle with ovulation on day 14.

Why LMP and not conception date? Because the exact date of ovulation and fertilization is rarely known, while the LMP date is usually remembered or recorded. Pregnancy is conventionally counted from the LMP, making it approximately 2 weeks longer than actual fetal age (from conception).

Step-by-step: Write down the first day of your last period → Add 280 days (40 weeks) → That is your estimated due date (EDD).

Naegele's Rule — The Classic Formula

Naegele's Rule is the mathematical formula behind LMP-based due date calculation, developed by German obstetrician Franz Karl Naegele in 1812 and still in universal use today:

Naegele's Rule: EDD = LMP + 1 year - 3 months + 7 days Example: LMP = January 15, 2026 Step 1: Add 1 year → January 15, 2027 Step 2: Minus 3 months → October 15, 2026 Step 3: Add 7 days → October 22, 2026 Due Date = October 22, 2026

Method 2: Known Conception Date

If you know the exact date of conception (e.g., from ovulation tracking, OPK strips, or fertility monitoring), you can calculate your due date more directly:

EDD = Conception Date + 266 days (38 weeks) Example: Conception date = February 1, 2026 EDD = February 1 + 266 days = October 25, 2026 Note: 266 days from conception = 280 days from LMP (assumes ovulation on day 14 of a 28-day cycle)

If your cycle is longer or shorter than 28 days, conception likely occurred on a different day relative to your LMP. For example, if your cycle is 35 days, ovulation likely occurred around day 21 — one week later than standard. Adjust accordingly by adding (cycle length - 28) days to your calculated EDD.

Method 3: Ultrasound Dating — Most Accurate

An early ultrasound (ideally performed between 7–13 weeks) provides the most accurate gestational age determination. The sonographer measures the Crown-Rump Length (CRL) — the distance from the top of the baby's head to its bottom — and compares it to established growth charts to calculate gestational age within ±5–7 days.

When should ultrasound dating override LMP dating? The ACOG (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists) recommends adjusting the EDD based on ultrasound if the discrepancy is:

  • Before 9 weeks: More than 5 days difference
  • 9–15 weeks 6 days: More than 7 days difference
  • 16–21 weeks 6 days: More than 10 days difference
  • 22 weeks or later: More than 14 days difference

Women with irregular menstrual cycles, unknown LMP, or PCOS often have more accurate dating from early ultrasound than from LMP calculation.

Method 4: IVF Transfer Date

For pregnancies achieved through In Vitro Fertilization (IVF), due date calculation is more precise because the exact date of fertilization and embryo age is known:

For Day 3 Embryo Transfer: EDD = Transfer Date + 263 days For Day 5 Embryo Transfer (Blastocyst): EDD = Transfer Date + 261 days Example: Day 5 blastocyst transferred on March 1, 2026 EDD = March 1 + 261 days = November 17, 2026

How Accurate Is Your Due Date?

Despite the precision of calculation, birth rarely happens on the estimated due date. Research consistently shows:

  • Only about 4% of babies are born on their exact due date
  • About 70% of births occur within 10 days of the EDD
  • About 90% of births occur within 2 weeks of the EDD
  • A "normal" delivery window is 37 weeks 0 days through 42 weeks 0 days

The due date is best understood as the center of a 5-week window (weeks 37–42) during which delivery is expected, not a precise deadline. Post-term pregnancies (beyond 42 weeks) are monitored carefully and often induced due to increasing placental insufficiency risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate my due date from my last period?
Add 280 days (40 weeks) to the first day of your last menstrual period. Alternatively, use Naegele's Rule: take your LMP date, add 1 year, subtract 3 months, and add 7 days. For example, if your LMP was January 1, your due date would be approximately October 8. Our free Due Date Calculator does this instantly.
How accurate is a due date calculated from LMP?
LMP-based due dates are accurate within about ±2 weeks for women with regular 28-day cycles. For women with irregular cycles, PCOS, or unknown LMP, accuracy is lower. An early ultrasound (7–13 weeks) is more accurate and can refine the due date to within ±5–7 days. Most providers use LMP for initial dating and confirm with early ultrasound.
What if my due date changes after an ultrasound?
It is very common for due dates to be adjusted after an early ultrasound. If the ultrasound measurement differs from the LMP-based date by more than 5–7 days in the first trimester, your provider will typically revise the due date. The revised date based on early ultrasound is usually more accurate. This does not indicate anything is wrong with your pregnancy.
Can I go into labor before my due date?
Yes. Any birth between 37 weeks 0 days and 41 weeks 6 days is considered normal timing. Births at 37–38 weeks are called early term, 39–40 weeks are full term, 41 weeks are late term, and 42+ weeks are post-term. About 11% of births in the US occur before 37 weeks (preterm). Signs of labor include regular contractions, water breaking, and bloody show.
